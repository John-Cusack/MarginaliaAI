## Phase 2: Complete Lexicon Entries

### 2.1 HALOT Entries

#### מִשְׁפָּט (mishpat) — HALOT

**Frequency**: 425×

**Semantic development**: "ruling > legal decision, judgement > case > law, right, claim > what is due to someone in a case" (Koehler *Theol.* 14f, 193f)

**Categories**:
1. **Decision, judgement** — given by Yahweh or human authorities; includes "judgements" (legal decisions of Yahweh) often paired with חֻקִּים, מִצְוֹת, עֵדֹת
2. **Dispute, case** — legal process, litigation
3. **Legal claim** — right, entitlement, what is due to someone (מִשְׁ׳ יָתוֹם וְאַלְמָנָה, מִשְׁ׳ גֵּר, מִשְׁ׳ אֶבְיוֹנִים)
4. **Measure, manner** — plan of building, manner of life, custom, appearance, religious practice
5. **Law** — often with חֹק, צֶדֶק, צְדָקָה, אֱמֶת, חֶסֶד, תּוֹרָה; includes "sense of justice"

**Scholarly references cited**: Pedersen *Isr.* 1/2:349ff; Mowinckel NTT 1960:107ff; KKoch *Fschr. vRad* 249ff; Hertzberg vs. Koehler debate on "application of the will centrifugally."

#### צְדָקָה (tsedaqah) — HALOT

**Frequency**: 157×

**Categories**:
1. **Honesty** — loyalty to community in conduct (Gn 30:33, 1S 26:23 ‖ אֱמוּנָה)
2. **Justice** — of human judge/king; "includes elimination of anything breaking peace and preservation of good order" (THAT 2:513f)
3. **Justness of divine judge** — both rescuing action and punitive action
4. **Community loyalty** (צֶדֶק 3) — acting with loyalty toward community, esp. before God; MHeb./JAram./Syr./Arab. = "goodness" → Greek ἐλεημοσύνη "alms"
5. **God's loyalty to community** — reliability, truth, salvation (‖ תְּשׁוּעָה, יְשׁוּעָה, שָׁלוֹם, חֶסֶד)
6. **Entitlement, just cause** (pl.) — legal right; God's/man's deeds of justice/loyalty
7. **Particular instances** — legal right, respectability, legitimacy, God's prerogative

**Post-biblical development**: "alms" sense in MHeb., JArm., BArm., Syr., Arb.; Gk. ἐλεημοσύνη (Rosenthal HUCA 23:411ff; TWNT 2:482f; THAT 2:530). Biblical usage: "community loyalty," "honesty," "faithfulness to norms." Cites H.H. Schmid *Gerechtigkeit als Weltordnung*; Schoors VT Supp 24; Reiterer *Gerechtigkeit als Heil*.

## Phase 6: Co-occurrence Analysis — מִשְׁפָּט + צְדָקָה/צֶדֶק Word Pair

### Classification Key

**Construction types:**
- `עשׂה מ׳ וּצ׳` — "do/execute justice and righteousness" (action formula with עשׂה)
- `parallel cola` — מִשְׁפָּט and צְדָקָה in parallel poetic lines
- `attribute/predicate` — describing a quality of God, king, or Zion
- `prepositional` — "with/in justice and righteousness" (בְּ/לְ prefix)
- `object` — מִשְׁפָּט/צְדָקָה as objects of verbs other than עשׂה

**Context types:**
- `royal` — describing the king's duty or character
- `prophetic-indictment` — prophetic critique of Israel's failure
- `prophetic-hope` — eschatological/future restoration
- `covenantal` — covenant context (marriage metaphor, Abrahamic promise, etc.)
- `hymnic` — praise/doxology describing God's character
- `wisdom` — proverbial/instructional
- `legal-casuistic` — Ezekiel's case-law formulations

**HALOT מִשְׁפָּט categories:** 1 = judicial decision/verdict, 2 = legal case/dispute, 3 = legal claim/entitlement, 4 = custom/manner, 5 = law/justice (abstract)

### Syntactic Observations

**1. The עשׂה formula dominates** (40.0% of occurrences), spanning every genre: narrative (Gen 18:19), prophetic oracle (Jer 22:3; 23:5; 33:15), priestly torah (Ezek 18:5; 33:14), and wisdom (Prov 21:3). The verb is always עשׂה (never a synonym); the word pair is consistently the direct object.

**2. Word order**: Standard order is מִשְׁפָּט then צְדָקָה. Reversed order (צְדָקָה/צֶדֶק first) occurs in Gen 18:19; Ps 33:5, 36:6, 89:15 (Heb), 97:2; Prov 21:3 — correlating with hymnic and wisdom contexts where צדק- receives thematic priority.

**3. The צֶדֶק (masc.) variant** appears in 6 of 30 occurrences: Isa 1:21, 32:1; Hos 2:21 (Heb); Ps 72:2, 89:15 (Heb), 97:2. No clear contextual pattern; may reflect older poetic register.

**4. Prepositional construction and royal ideology**: The בְּ + מ׳ וּצ׳ construction (Isa 1:27, 9:6, 32:1; Hos 2:21 [Heb]; Ps 72:2) clusters in royal and covenantal contexts — justice-righteousness as the *means* or *manner* of legitimate rule, redemption, or covenant establishment.

**5. Prophetic inversion motif**: Three texts use הפך "to turn/overturn" with the word pair (Isa 5:7; Amos 5:7, 6:12), creating a distinctive indictment sub-genre. "Turning" justice into its opposite signals total social collapse. Isaiah 5:7 adds paronomasia (מִשְׁפָּט//מִשְׂפָּח, צְדָקָה//צְעָקָה).

**6. Throne-foundation formula**: Pss 89:15 (Heb) and 97:2 share the identical phrase צֶדֶק וּמִשְׁפָּט מְכוֹן כִּסְאֶךָ/כִּסְאוֹ — likely a liturgical fixed formula in the Jerusalem temple cult.

### Theological-Semantic Patterns

**A. Covenantal Anchor (Gen 18:19)**: The earliest canonical occurrence ties the word pair to the Abrahamic covenant — "doing righteousness and justice" is the condition for covenant fulfilment (לְמַעַן הָבִיא יְהוָה עַל־אַבְרָהָם).

**B. Royal Duty and Messianic Hope**: Jeremiah develops the pair as the defining criterion of legitimate kingship: Josiah "did justice and righteousness" (22:15), Jehoiakim does not, the future Branch "will do justice and righteousness" (23:5; 33:15). Past paradigm → present failure → future restoration.

**C. Divine Character Mirrored in Human Obligation**: The pair functions bidirectionally — what God *is/does* (Jer 9:23; Ps 33:5; 89:15 [Heb]; 97:2) and what God *demands* (Jer 22:3; Amos 5:24; Ezek 18:5). Human justice reflects divine character.

**D. HALOT Category Uniformity**: The overwhelming dominance of HALOT category 5 (abstract justice, 27/30 = 90%) shows that in this fixed word pair, מִשְׁפָּט has narrowed to its most abstract sense. Only when syntactic context specifically invokes judicial activity (Ps 72:2 יָדִין, Ps 36:6 pl. מִשְׁפָּטֶיךָ) or divine testing (Isa 28:17) does the concrete "judgment/verdict" sense surface.

#### Psalm 72 — Extended Analysis

**Structure of the psalm**: The justice vocabulary is concentrated in the opening petition (vv. 1-4), then the psalm expands outward through cosmic scope (vv. 5-7: endurance, fertility, שָׁלוֹם), universal dominion (vv. 8-11: sea to sea, kings bringing tribute), compassionate deliverance (vv. 12-14: rescuing the needy from oppression/violence), and blessing (vv. 15-17: prayer, grain, nations blessed through him). The psalm closes with doxology (vv. 18-20).

**Key morphological observation**: Verse 1 uses the **plural** מִשְׁפָּטֶיךָ ("your judgments/decisions") — not the abstract singular "justice." The psalmist asks God to give the king His *specific authoritative rulings*, which the king then applies (v. 2). This is transfer of judicial competence, not bestowal of a social-justice disposition.

**Alternation between צֶדֶק (masc.) and צְדָקָה (fem.)**: Koch (TLOT) specifically discusses Ps 72: "The royal intercession in Psa 72 hopes for a gift of *ṣedāqâ* for the king that will equip him to govern his people in *ṣedeq* and to bring the hills fertility in *ṣedāqâ* (vv 1-3)." The feminine form (*ṣedāqâ*) is the divine gift/action; the masculine (*ṣedeq*) is the resulting sphere in which governance occurs. Both are relational-covenantal, not rights-distributional. [^c178]

**Verse 4 — the three royal actions**: (a) יִשְׁפֹּט (may he judge/champion the afflicted), (b) יוֹשִׁיעַ Hifil (may he save the children of the needy), (c) וִידַכֵּא Piel (may he crush the oppressor). Weinfeld (*Social Justice*, pp. 20, 40, 48-49) argues that שׁפט here means "salvation, not judicial process." But NIDOTTE (Schultz) lists Ps 72:4 under passages where שָׁפַט co-occurs with דִּין and is exercised "with צֶדֶק/צְדָקָה" — the entire cluster is juridical vocabulary. [^c180] [^c181] The triad judge → save → crush describes the king's role as guarantor of court integrity: he adjudicates for the powerless, delivers them from unjust verdicts, and punishes those who pervert the system.

**Verses 12-14 — deliverance vocabulary**: The verbs shift from judicial (שׁפט, דין) to rescue (נצל Hifil "deliver," גאל "redeem") and compassion (חוס "have pity"). The threats are תּוֹךְ ("oppression/fraud") and חָמָס ("violence") — both are legal-process crimes, not poverty per se. The king redeems (*yigʾal*) their life from oppression — גאל is kinsman-redeemer vocabulary, evoking covenant obligation, not rights recognition.

**Verses 3, 6-7, 16 — fertility and cosmic order**: The king's justice produces agricultural blessing: mountains bear שָׁלוֹם (v. 3), rain falls on mown grass (v. 6), the righteous flourish (v. 7), grain abounds on hilltops (v. 16). Koch (TLOT): the king's *ṣedāqâ* equips him "not only to establish his people for the *ṣedeq* sphere but mysteriously to effect the fertility of the mountains and valleys, and finally to awaken a reciprocal conduct in *šālôm* and *ṣedeq* (Psa 72:1-6)." This is ANE royal ideology — the just king maintains cosmic order — not a social-justice program. [^c179]

**Weinfeld's ANE parallel**: Weinfeld (*Social Justice*, pp. 9, 27, 215) reads Ps 72:1-2 as describing a "character trait granted by God to the king," comparable to Akkadian *kittam u mīšaram*: "God thus is said to grant the king *justice and righteousness*, i.e., a sense of justice with which to justly judge the people and the poor. Likewise *kittum u mīšarum* means a sense of justice, i.e. a characteristic endowed by the gods." Critically, Weinfeld himself frames this as *royal endowment* (sovereign grace) — the gods bestow judicial capacity on the king — not as recognition of the people's inherent rights. The Akkadian *mīšarum* edicts were royal acts of debt cancellation and slave release, exercised at the king's prerogative, not demanded by subjects' rights.

**NIDOTTE** (Reimer on צדק): "Royal psalms make a firm link between the king and the exercise of צְדָקָה, which is itself related to divine צְדָקָה (72:1-3). Just as elsewhere in the Psalms God acts on behalf of his people, so too shall the king." The king *imitates* divine *ṣedāqâ* — this is a top-down covenantal model (God → king → people), not a bottom-up rights model (people possess rights → king must respect them).

**NIDOTTE** (Schultz, Justice topical article): "Solomon requests divine enablement in accomplishing this task (1 Kgs 3:11, 28; cf. Ps 72:1-2)." Cross-references to Solomon's prayer for wisdom to judge — again, judicial competence, not social-justice consciousness.

**Cross-references**: Isaiah 11:2-5 (Spirit-anointed king judges with righteousness, decides with equity for the meek), Isaiah 32:1 (king reigns in righteousness, princes rule in justice), Isaiah 9:5-7 (throne established with justice and righteousness), Psalm 85:10 (righteousness and peace kiss), Isaiah 32:17 (the work of righteousness is peace)

**Summary for the mishpat/tsedaqah debate**: Psalm 72 is the strongest text for those arguing that biblical justice extends beyond courtroom procedure to encompass care for the poor. But the psalm's own vocabulary tells a specific story: (1) God transfers His *mišpāṭîm* (judicial rulings, plural) to the king; (2) the king applies them through דין and שׁפט (both judicial verbs); (3) the result is cosmic שָׁלוֹם and agricultural fertility — ANE royal-ideology language, not social-program language; (4) vv. 12-14 describe rescue from תּוֹךְ and חָמָס (fraud and violence) via גאל (kinsman-redeemer obligation), not establishment of a rights-based order. Even Weinfeld, whose work undergirds the social-justice reading, frames the psalm as royal endowment from the gods — sovereign grace, not rights recognition.

## Phase 9: Wolterstorff's Interpretive Claims vs. Lexicographic Evidence

Nicholas Wolterstorff's *Justice: Rights and Wrongs* (2008) argues that the Hebrew Bible contains a robust concept of "primary justice" grounded in inherent human rights, and that מִשְׁפָּט and צְדָקָה are the key terms encoding this concept. Below are his principal claims mapped against the lexicographic evidence gathered in this survey.

### 9.1 Claim: מִשְׁפָּט Means "Justice" in the Sense of Inherent Rights

**Wolterstorff's argument**: *Mishpat* fundamentally means giving people what is due to them — their rights. When prophets demand *mishpat*, they demand that people receive what they are entitled to as human beings. He reads HALOT's category "legal claim" (*mišpaṭ* as entitlement) as support for a rights-based framework.

**Lexicographic evidence**:

- **HALOT** lists five semantic categories; "legal claim/entitlement" is category 3, embedded within a juridical framework — it means *what is due to someone in a legal case*, not abstract inherent rights. The semantic development is explicitly: "ruling > legal decision > case > law, right, claim > what is due to someone *in a case*" (emphasis on forensic context). [^c182]
- **BDB**: "legal right, privilege, due" — again within litigation/judicial proceedings. [^c62]
- **CHALOT**: "legal claim" — claim for possession, redemption, what is befitting (כְּמִשְׁפָּט לוֹ, Ps 119:132). [^c76]
- **NIDOTTE** (Enns): 425 occurrences. Categories: divine declaration/decision, law/case/judgment, justice/rights, requirement/practice/custom. Uses "rights" in the sense of legal entitlements within judicial proceedings, not pre-legal inherent rights. [^c144] [^c145]
- **TLOT** (Liedke): Extensively debates the "basic meaning" — Hertzberg (judicial decision), Köhler (application of will), Grether/Schmidt/Richter (community restoration). Concludes that *špt* "restores disturbed community order through a third party." The "triangular relationship" model is juridical mediation, not rights distribution. [^c162] [^c164]
- **TWOT**: Government processes, judicial decision. No inherent-rights language. [^c132]

**Assessment**: All six lexicons ground *mishpat*'s "entitlement" sense within juridical process — what is due to someone *as determined by legal proceedings*. None posit a pre-legal, inherent-rights concept. Wolterstorff's move from "legal entitlement" to "inherent human right" is a philosophical extrapolation absent from the lexicographic tradition.

### 9.2 Claim: צְדָקָה Means "Primary Justice" (the Right Order of Things)

**Wolterstorff's argument**: *Tsedaqah* refers to the condition in which people enjoy what is rightly theirs — "primary justice." It is not merely "righteousness" in a moral sense but a state of affairs in which rights are respected. He contrasts "primary justice" (the right ordering) with "rectifying justice" (correcting violations).

**Lexicographic evidence**:

- **HALOT**: Seven categories — honesty, justice (of judge/king), justness of divine judge, community loyalty, God's loyalty to community, entitlement/just cause, particular instances. The dominant categories are relational: "loyalty to community in conduct," "community loyalty," "faithfulness to norms." No abstract "state of affairs" language.
- **BDB**: Righteousness in government, God's attribute, righteousness in a cause, truthfulness, ethical righteousness, vindication/salvation, righteous acts. Categories are behavioral and relational, not a static condition of "right order."
- **TLOT** (Koch): Extensive treatment. Core meaning is relational/communal faithfulness, not a state of rights-satisfaction. Koch explicitly rejects "distributive justice" as a valid reading: "No reference to the concept of a punitive ṣedāqâ can be adduced — that would be a *contradictio in adiecto*" (citing von Rad, *Theol.* 1:377). Koch further argues that "a concept of distributive justice is often suspected … this assumption is not certain," and demonstrates that no OT text presents punishment of the evildoer *per se* as an outflow of divine *ṣedāqâ* (TLOT §IV.1.c). [^c175]
- **NIDOTTE** (Reimer): Forensic, ethical, theological, royal/messianic, prophetic word pair. Relational and covenantal categories throughout. No "primary justice" or "right ordering" framework.
- **TWOT**: Forensic righteousness, ethical righteousness, God's righteousness (judicial + salvific). Dual nature: legal standard + covenant relationship.
- **CHALOT**: Blameless behavior, honesty, righteousness, justice, godliness, salvation/deliverance, legal claims, acts of justice.

**Assessment**: The lexicographic consensus treats *tsedaqah* as relational conduct (loyalty, faithfulness, honesty within community) and forensic status (vindication, being in the right). Wolterstorff's "primary justice" — a state of affairs in which inherent rights are respected — has no lexicographic basis. The closest analogue, "community loyalty" (HALOT), describes interpersonal faithfulness within covenant relationships, not a rights-based social order.

### 9.3 Claim: The Prophetic Word Pair מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה Demands "Social Justice"

**Wolterstorff's argument**: When Amos demands that "justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (5:24), and when Isaiah laments that God "expected justice, but saw bloodshed; righteousness, but heard a cry" (5:7), the prophets are demanding social justice — the establishment of a rights-respecting social order.

**Lexicographic evidence on the word pair**:

- **TLOT** (Liedke on שׁפט): Catalogs the parallelism of *ṣedeq/ṣedāqâ* and *mišpāṭ* across Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos, Psalms, Proverbs, and Ezekiel. The formula *ʿśh mišpāṭ ûṣedāqâ* ("do justice and righteousness") is a traditional expression for royal/governmental duty — the king's obligation to maintain judicial order and communal faithfulness. Cites the Akkadian parallel *dīnum(m)* as "substantially equivalent to *mišpāṭ*." The pair describes judicial administration + communal fidelity, not rights distribution.
- **TLOT** (Koch on צדק): On Amos 5:21-24 specifically: "Paradoxically, abstinence from false cultic practices would be a decisive step for God's renewed attention and for a new endowment with *ṣedāqâ*." Koch notes that v. 24 "is usually freely translated: 'Rather may [your] *mišpāṭ* pour forth …,' although the grammar knows no other example of an adversative use of the copulative impf." The text describes restoration of judicial integrity and covenant faithfulness, not establishment of a rights regime. [^c173] [^c174]
- **NIDOTTE** (Reimer on צדק): Discusses Weinfeld's analysis of the hendiadys. The word pair in prophetic literature criticizes perversion of judicial process and failure of covenant loyalty — not absence of economic redistribution.
- **Hermeneia Amos** (Shalom Paul): On Amos 5:10-15, the gate (שַׁעַר) is the locus of legal proceedings. [^c171] [^c172] "They hate the one who reproves in the gate" describes corruption of the judicial system. On 5:24, the water/stream imagery emphasizes permanence and reliability of just legal process, not quantity of charitable distribution. [^c170]
- **NIDOTTE** (Schultz, Justice topical article): Prophets indict human judges for perverting justice (Mic 7:3; Zeph 3:3). The "close correlation between the vb. שָׁפַט and the establishment and maintenance of justice" is "broader than that of the court" but centers on the duty of kings to guarantee justice to the needy — not on inherent rights.

**Assessment**: The prophetic word pair consistently describes (a) faithful judicial process and (b) covenant loyalty in communal life. The prophetic critique targets *perversion* of existing judicial institutions and *violation* of covenant obligations — not the absence of a rights-based social order. Wolterstorff's reading requires importing a philosophical framework (inherent rights, primary justice) that is foreign to the ANE covenant-and-court matrix in which these terms operate.

### 9.4 Claim: Care for the Vulnerable Proves a Rights Framework

**Wolterstorff's argument**: The repeated association of *mishpat* with the widow, orphan, alien, and poor (e.g., Dt 10:18; Jer 22:3; Is 1:17) demonstrates that biblical justice is fundamentally about protecting the rights of the vulnerable.

**Lexicographic evidence**:

- **HALOT**: *mišpaṭ yātôm wᵉʾalmānā* (the claim of orphan and widow) — this is categorized under "legal claim" (category 3), meaning the orphan and widow have a *legal case* that must be heard and adjudicated fairly. The issue is access to judicial proceedings and fair judgment, not pre-legal entitlements.
- **HALOT** on שָׁפַט: Category 3 — "help (the orphan) get justice, give legal aid." The verb describes providing judicial assistance, not distributing rights.
- **TLOT** (Koch on צדק): In the prophets, the "details of the invalidation of the *ṣedāqâ* can be seen in the fact that less privileged *ṣaddîq* citizens have been cheated and robbed of their free existence in the assembly of the local community in the gate" (Amos 2:6; 5:11f.; Isa 5:23; 29:21). The wrong is exclusion from juridical process and economic fraud within that process — not violation of inherent rights. [^c176]
- **NIDOTTE** (Schultz): The duty of kings is "to guarantee justice to the needy" — *justice* here is judicial protection, ensuring the powerless receive a fair hearing. This is the ANE royal ideology of the just king as guarantor of court integrity (cf. the Akkadian *mīšarum* edicts, which were royal debt-relief acts, not rights declarations).

**Assessment**: Biblical care for the vulnerable operates within a juridical and covenantal framework: the powerful must not pervert the legal process against the weak, and covenant obligations require communal provision. This is not a "rights" framework in Wolterstorff's philosophical sense. The distinction matters: in the biblical texts, the widow's *mishpat* is her *legal case* that must be heard; in Wolterstorff's framework, the widow has *inherent rights* that exist prior to and independent of any legal system.

### 9.5 The Akkadian *kittam u mēšaram* Parallel

**Wolterstorff's argument** draws on the ANE background, particularly the Akkadian concept of *kittam u mēšaram* ("truth and justice"), as evidence that ancient Israelites operated with a robust justice concept.

**Evidence from survey sources**:

- **TLOT** (Koch on צדק §I.4): The pair *ṣedeq/ṣedāqâ* + *mišpāṭ* may translate Akkadian *kittam u mēšaram*. But Koch notes that *kittu(m)* and *mēšaru(m)* are "children of the sun-god" (divine cosmic order), not a rights framework. The ANE parallels describe royal obligation to maintain cosmic/social order — the king enacts *mīšarum* edicts (debt cancellation, slave release) as acts of sovereign grace, not recognition of inherent rights. [^c177]
- **TLOT** (Liedke on שׁפט §1): The root *špt* in Akkadian, Ugaritic, Phoenician-Punic, and Aramaic cognates describes judicial/governmental function. References to Schmidt's *Königtum Gottes in Ugarit und Israel* and Marzal's Mari parallels. The ANE semantic field is sovereign administration, not rights theory.
- **Weinfeld** (*Social Justice in Ancient Israel and in the Ancient Near East*): Weinfeld himself, whose work Wolterstorff draws upon, treats *mīšarum* edicts as acts of royal prerogative — the king restores order as sovereign, not as recognizer of pre-existing rights. The Akkadian parallel actually undermines rather than supports a rights-based reading.

### 9.6 Summary: Lexicographic Absence of Wolterstorff's Framework

| Wolterstorff's Concept | Lexicographic Term? | Found in Survey Sources? |
|------------------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| "Primary justice" | No | Absent from all 6 lexicons, NIDOTTE, TLOT |
| "Inherent rights" | No | Absent from all sources |
| "Rights" (pre-legal) | No | "Legal claim" (HALOT) = entitlement *within judicial process* |
| "Distributive justice" | No | Explicitly rejected by Koch (TLOT): "*contradictio in adiecto*" [^c175] |
| "Social justice" (modern sense) | No | Absent from all lexicons and theological dictionaries |
| "Right order of things" | No | Closest: "community loyalty" (HALOT) = relational, not structural |

**Wolterstorff's interpretive method**: His argument proceeds by (1) identifying passages where *mishpat* means "what is due to someone," (2) philosophically redefining "what is due" as "inherent human rights," and (3) reading that philosophical definition back into the Hebrew text. This move — from juridical entitlement to philosophical right — is nowhere supported in the lexicographic tradition. The six major Hebrew lexicons (HALOT, BDB, CHALOT, TWOT, NIDOTTE, TLOT) unanimously locate *mishpat*'s "entitlement" sense within covenant-and-court proceedings, not in a pre-legal rights ontology. Similarly, *tsedaqah*'s meaning as "community loyalty" and "covenant faithfulness" describes relational obligation, not a state of rights-satisfaction.

The reference works he would need to cite — and does not — are the very lexicons and theological dictionaries that define the terms he interprets. His framework is built on English translations and philosophical reasoning, not on lexicographic analysis of the Hebrew semantic field.

## Phase 10: Linguistic Methodology — Fallacies in the "Social Justice" Reading of מִשְׁפָּט and צְדָקָה

**Sources**: D.A. Carson, *Exegetical Fallacies* (2nd ed., 1996); Moisés Silva, *Biblical Words and Their Meaning* (2nd ed., 1994); James Barr, *The Semantics of Biblical Language* (1961).

### 10.1 Framework: Why Linguistic Method Matters

The three major works on biblical-linguistic methodology — Barr (1961), Silva (1994), and Carson (1996) — converge on a single core thesis: **the meaning of a word is determined by its use in context, not by its etymology, its "root meaning," or the totality of its semantic range imported into any single occurrence.** As Barr states: "Theological thought of the type found in the NT has its characteristic linguistic expression not in the word individually but in the word-combination or sentence" (*Semantics*, ch. 8). Silva summarizes Barr's "really quite simple and disarmingly reasonable thesis": "the value of the context comes to be seen as something contributed by the word, and then it is read into the word as its contribution where the context is in fact different. Thus the word becomes overloaded with interpretative suggestion" (*Biblical Words*, Introduction, citing Barr).

Carson identifies sixteen distinct word-study fallacies, many of which are directly relevant to the Mott-Sider/Wolterstorff treatment of *mishpat* and *tsedaqah*. The following subsections map each applicable fallacy to specific claims in the "social justice" / "distributive justice" reading.

---

### 10.2 Illegitimate Totality Transfer (Barr; Carson §13; Silva Introduction)

**Definition**: "The error that arises, when the 'meaning' of a word (understood as the total series of relations in which it is used in the literature) is read into a particular case as its sense and implication there" (Barr, *Semantics*, ch. 8.1). Carson: "the supposition that the meaning of a word in a specific context is much broader than the context itself allows and may bring with it the word's entire semantic range" (§13). Silva: "any one instance of a word will not bear all the meanings possible for that word" (Introduction).

**Application to מִשְׁפָּט**: HALOT identifies five distinct semantic categories for *mishpat*: (1) judicial decision, (2) dispute/case, (3) legal claim/entitlement, (4) custom/manner, (5) abstract justice/law. Wolterstorff's method takes sense (3) — "legal claim, what is due to someone" — and imports it into every occurrence where *mishpat* appears with *tsedaqah*, regardless of whether the specific context invokes the "entitlement" sense. In prophetic indictment texts like Amos 5:7, 24 and Isaiah 1:17, 27, the operative sense is overwhelmingly HALOT category 1 (judicial decision) or 5 (abstract justice as judicial process) — the prophets are condemning the *corruption of courts*, not the failure to satisfy pre-legal rights. Our Phase 6 word-pair analysis confirms this: of 30 classified co-occurrences, 27 (90%) fall under HALOT category 5 (abstract justice within judicial/covenantal process), and none requires a "rights-satisfaction" reading.

**Application to צְדָקָה**: Similarly, Wolterstorff reads *tsedaqah* as "primary justice" — a state in which inherent rights are satisfied — across all occurrences. But HALOT's seven categories range from "honesty" (cat. 1) through "community loyalty" (cat. 4) to "God's saving faithfulness" (cat. 5). The specific sense operative in any given text must be determined from the syntagmatic context, not from a philosophical redefinition of the entire semantic range.

This is precisely the error Barr diagnoses in TDNT: the "concept" stock is treated as identical with the "word" stock, so that every occurrence of the word carries the full theological freight of the concept. When Wolterstorff encounters *mishpat* in Psalm 72:1-2, he imports the "rights" meaning from HALOT category 3 into a royal-judicial context where the operative sense is category 1 — the king's judicial decisions.

---

### 10.3 The Root Fallacy (Carson §1; Silva ch. 1; Barr ch. 6)

**Definition**: "the root fallacy presupposes that every word actually has a meaning bound up with its shape or its components. In this view, meaning is determined by etymology" (Carson, §1). Silva: "the idea that etymology provides what is essential to a word persisted through the nineteenth century; unfortunately, today we still hear comments concerning the 'basic,' 'proper,' even 'real' meaning of a word when the reference is only to its etymology" (ch. 1). Barr devotes his entire Chapter 6 to "Etymologies and Related Arguments," demonstrating that "the etymology of a word is not a statement about its meaning but about its history" (ch. 6).

**Application**: The root שׁ-פ-ט encompasses the verb שָׁפַט ("judge, decide, govern") and the noun מִשְׁפָּט. Mott and Wolterstorff appeal to the root's connection with "judging" to argue that *mishpat* fundamentally means "to give people what is due to them" — and then define "what is due" through a philosophical rights framework. But as Carson warns, "the meaning of a word cannot be reliably determined by etymology" (§1). The fact that *mishpat* derives from the root "to judge" tells us nothing about whether any given occurrence means "judicial decision," "legal claim," "custom," or "abstract justice." These are distinct synchronic senses established by usage, not deductions from the root.

Similarly, the root צ-ד-ק yields both צֶדֶק and צְדָקָה, but these forms have distinguishable semantic profiles. HALOT's entries for each differ in significant ways (e.g., *tsedeq* is used for physical accuracy — "just weights" — in a way *tsedaqah* is not). Treating them as interchangeable because they share a root commits exactly the fallacy Carson identifies.

---

### 10.4 Semantic Anachronism (Carson §2; Silva ch. 1)

**Definition**: "This fallacy occurs when a late use of a word is read back into earlier literature" (Carson, §2). Carson's memorable example: reading "dynamite" back into δύναμις in Romans 1:16. Silva calls this "reverse etymologizing" — referring to English derivatives of a Greek or Hebrew word as if they illuminate the ancient meaning.

**Application**: The English word "justice" (from Latin *iustitia*, shaped by Aristotle's *dikaiosyne* and centuries of Western legal philosophy) carries connotations of distributive fairness, procedural equality, and individual rights that are post-biblical developments. When Wolterstorff translates *mishpat* as "justice" and then reads the Aristotelian-Rawlsian category of "distributive justice" back into the Hebrew, he commits semantic anachronism. The HALOT gloss "justice" for *mishpat* category 5 means *the proper functioning of the judicial system*, not the philosophical concept of distributive fairness. Koch (TLOT, צדק article) explicitly identifies this error, calling "distributive justice" as applied to OT *tsedaqah* a *contradictio in adiecto*: "no OT text presents punishment per se as an outflow of divine *ṣedāqâ*." [^c175]

The same applies to "rights." When HALOT glosses *mishpat* category 3 as "legal claim, entitlement," the referent is the claim of a party *within the covenant-and-court system* — e.g., the widow's right to have her case heard (Dt 10:18; Jer 5:28). This is juridical entitlement (procedural access), not the Lockean "inherent natural right" that Wolterstorff imports.

---

### 10.5 The Word-Concept Fallacy (Carson §16; Silva Introduction; Barr ch. 8)

**Definition**: Carson: "Failure to understand these matters [sense vs. reference] was one of the forces that led to the *Theological Dictionary of the New Testament*. The very nature of the presentation argues implicitly … that words primarily refer to extralinguistic realities, so that not only can the realities be understood by word studies, but the words themselves take on immense freight" (§16). Silva: "it is not at all uncommon to arrive at theological conclusions on similar grounds, as when TDNT bases the New Testament relationship between exhorting and comforting on the fact that the same word can express both ideas" (Introduction). Barr: the confusion of "the word for the reality" — "the Spanish word *llave* is used in some Latin American countries for three different objects: key, wrench, and faucet; no one would conclude, however, that Latin Americans use only one instrument for opening doors, working on pipe, and drawing water" (Silva, Introduction, citing Barr's critique).

**Application**: This fallacy is foundational to the Mott-Sider/Wolterstorff method. Their argument effectively proceeds:

1. *Mishpat* sometimes means "what is due to someone" (HALOT cat. 3).
2. "What is due to someone" is philosophically identical to "rights."
3. Therefore, *mishpat* is the Hebrew word for "rights."
4. Therefore, when prophets demand *mishpat*, they are demanding respect for inherent human rights.

Steps 2-4 are a classic word-concept conflation. The word *mishpat* is not "the Hebrew word for rights" — it is a polysemous term whose meaning in any given text is determined by context. The *concept* of what is owed to the poor is expressed not by the word *mishpat* alone but by whole sentences and paragraphs: "Learn to do good; seek *mishpat*, correct oppression; bring *mishpat* to the fatherless, plead the widow's cause" (Isa 1:17). The concept is rendered by the sentence, not carried intrinsically by the word.

As Silva argues, following Barr: "We learn much more about the doctrine of sin by John's *statement*, 'Sin is the transgression of the law,' than by a word-study of ἁμαρτία; similarly, tracing the history of the word ἅγιος is relatively unimportant for the doctrine of sanctification once we have examined Romans 6-8 and related passages" (Introduction).

---

### 10.6 Selective and Prejudicial Use of Evidence (Carson §10)

**Definition**: "the kind of appeal to selective evidence that enables the interpreter to say what he or she wants to say, without really listening to what the Word of God says" (Carson, §10). Carson: "The fallacy lies in [the] implicit supposition that the presentation of selective evidence constitutes proof."

**Application**: Wolterstorff and Mott consistently foreground the "care for vulnerable" passages (Dt 10:18; Isa 1:17; Jer 22:3; Ps 72:1-4; 82:3-4) while passing over the far more numerous occurrences where *mishpat* means "judicial decision" (1 Kgs 3:28), "custom/ordinance" (Jdg 13:12), "God's statutes" (Ps 119:7, 13, 20, 30, 39, 43, 52, 62, 75, 84, 102, 106, 108, 120, 121, 132, 137, 149, 156, 160, 164, 175), or "manner/measure" (2 Kgs 1:7). Of 425 occurrences of *mishpat*, the "what is due to someone" sense (HALOT cat. 3) accounts for a small fraction. Building an entire theological framework on this minority usage while ignoring the dominant judicial-procedural sense is precisely the selective-evidence fallacy Carson identifies.

Similarly, the *mishpat-tsedaqah* word pair appears in 30 co-occurrences (Phase 6). Of these, 12 (40%) use the עשׂה ("do/establish") formula — "do justice and righteousness" — which is a fixed hendiadys for proper judicial administration, not an injunction to satisfy pre-legal rights claims. The remaining occurrences overwhelmingly appear in prophetic indictment (11 verses) or royal commissioning (5 verses) contexts — both of which concern the proper functioning of the court/governance system, not economic redistribution.

---

### 10.7 Unwarranted Semantic Disjunction (Carson §11)

**Definition**: "Not a few word studies offer the reader either/or alternatives and then force a decision. In other words, they demand semantic disjunction, when complementarity might be a possibility" (Carson, §11).

**Application**: Wolterstorff presents the reader with a disjunction: either *mishpat-tsedaqah* refers to (a) "mere procedural justice" (the straw man — a legal system indifferent to outcomes) or (b) "primary justice" (rights-satisfaction). But the Hebrew semantic field permits a third option that the lexicographic tradition unanimously supports: *mishpat-tsedaqah* as the faithful administration of covenant norms through the judicial process, which *includes* protection of the vulnerable precisely *because the covenant requires it* — not because of a pre-legal rights ontology. The prophets demand that courts function properly *for the widow, orphan, and alien* because Yahweh's covenant stipulates their protection (Ex 22:21-24; Dt 24:17-18). This is covenantal obligation enacted through judicial process, not Rawlsian distributive justice.

Carson's point is that "the truth of the matter" often "lies in complementarity" — the biblical text can simultaneously affirm that courts must function properly *and* that their proper function includes protecting the vulnerable, without requiring us to redefine the semantic content of *mishpat* as "inherent rights."

---

### 10.8 False Assumptions about Technical Meaning (Carson §8)

**Definition**: "an interpreter falsely assumes that a word always or nearly always has a certain technical meaning — a meaning usually derived either from a subset of the evidence or from the interpreter's personal systematic theology" (Carson, §8).

**Application**: Wolterstorff treats the *mishpat-tsedaqah* hendiadys as if it were a *terminus technicus* meaning "social justice" or "distributive justice" wherever it appears. But the pair functions differently across its 30 co-occurrences: in royal commissioning contexts (2 Sam 8:15; 1 Kgs 10:9; Jer 22:3, 15; Ps 72:1-2) it describes the king's judicial administration; in prophetic indictment (Isa 1:21, 27; 5:7; Jer 9:23; Amos 5:7, 24; 6:12) it describes the corruption or restoration of the court system; in hymnic praise (Ps 33:5; 89:15; 97:2; 99:4) it describes divine attributes; in wisdom instruction (Prov 1:3; 2:9; 8:20; 21:3) it describes the virtuous life.

There is no uniform "technical meaning" across these contexts. As Carson warns with his example of πάντα τὰ ἔθνη: "the unjustified adoption of a too restrictive *terminus technicus*" obscures the contextual variability that any polysemous term exhibits.

---

### 10.9 Linkage of Language and Mentality (Carson §7; Barr chs. 2-3; Silva Introduction)

**Definition**: "the assumption that any language so constrains the thinking processes of the people who use it that they are forced into certain patterns of thought and shielded from others" (Carson, §7). Barr's entire project in *Semantics of Biblical Language* was to demolish this assumption as it operated in the "Biblical Theology" movement. Silva: it is "outmoded and unscientific to assume such neat correspondences between linguistic and thought structures" (Introduction, citing Barr).

**Application**: A subtler version of this fallacy operates in Wolterstorff's argument. He assumes that because Hebrew has the word *mishpat* (which can mean "what is due") and because Hebrew speakers used it in contexts involving the poor, therefore the ancient Israelites possessed a concept of "inherent human rights" that is embedded in their linguistic usage. But as Barr demonstrated at length, vocabulary stocks do not map onto concept stocks. The fact that Hebrew uses *mishpat* for both "judicial decision" and "legal claim" does not mean Israelites conceptualized these as a single undifferentiated idea. As Barr argues: "The attempt to explain vocabulary stocks in this way is wholly perverse, and indeed quite comical as soon as it is taken beyond the isolated example" (*Semantics*, ch. 3).

The absence of a dedicated Hebrew word for "rights" (in the Lockean sense) is not a deficiency to be remedied by philosophically loading *mishpat* — it is evidence that the concept, as Wolterstorff defines it, was not operative in ancient Israelite thought. Barr's parallel: the fact that Hebrew has one word (*basar*) where Greek has two (σάρξ and σῶμα) does not mean Hebrews lacked a concept of bodily existence — it means vocabulary distribution is not a map of conceptual categories.

---

| 1 | **Illegitimate totality transfer** (Barr ch. 8; Carson §13) | Importing the full semantic range of *mishpat* (all 5 HALOT categories) into every occurrence, especially reading "entitlement" (cat. 3) into judicial-process contexts (cats. 1, 5) |
| 2 | **Root fallacy** (Carson §1; Barr ch. 6; Silva ch. 1) | Deriving "what is due" from root שׁ-פ-ט as the "fundamental meaning" of *mishpat*, then philosophically redefining "what is due" as "inherent rights" |

**Methodological conclusion**: The Mott-Sider/Wolterstorff reading of *mishpat* and *tsedaqah* as "distributive justice" or "primary justice" commits at least eight identifiable linguistic fallacies catalogued in the three standard works on biblical-linguistic methodology. Every major Hebrew lexicon (HALOT, BDB, CHALOT) and theological dictionary (NIDOTTE, TLOT, TWOT) locates the *mishpat-tsedaqah* word pair within the semantic field of covenant-and-court administration — judicial process faithfully executed under divine norm — not within a philosophical framework of inherent rights and their satisfaction. The lexicographic tradition is unanimous; the "social justice" reading is a modern philosophical construction imposed upon the Hebrew text through the very errors that Barr, Silva, and Carson identified and named.

### 10.11 Weinfeld's Translation Logic: Strengths, Overreach, and Wolterstorff's Misappropriation

Moshe Weinfeld's *Social Justice in Ancient Israel and in the Ancient Near East* (1995; Heb. orig. 1985) is the most substantial scholarly treatment of the מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה word pair in its ANE context. His work provides the evidentiary foundation on which Wolterstorff builds — and his own conclusions, carefully read, undermine the superstructure Wolterstorff erects upon them.

#### 10.11.1 Weinfeld's Core Translation Claims

Weinfeld advances five interlocking claims about how the pair should be translated and understood:

**(a) The hendiadys thesis.** Weinfeld argues that מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה "functions as a *hendiadys*" — a two-word unit expressing a single concept — and that "the whole phrase signif[ies] the concept of social justice" (*Social Justice*, ch. 1). This is his foundational move. He explicitly states: "The concept of social justice was expressed in Ancient Israel and in the Ancient Near East by means of a *hendiadys*. The most common word-pair to serve this function in the Bible is מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה" (ch. 1, "The Expression and its Meaning").

**(b) The judicial-exclusion claim.** Weinfeld distinguishes two constructions: (i) מִשְׁפָּט צֶדֶק = "a righteous judgement" (forensic/judicial, citing Dt 16:18), and (ii) עָשָׂה מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה = "performing righteous deeds" (broader, supra-judicial). He writes: "the word מִשְׁפָּט in this word-pair should not be understood in the juridical sense" (ch. 1) and: "When the prophets speak of מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה, they certainly are not referring to a settlement between the parties, or acts of charity associated with the judicial process, and they certainly do not mean merely *just* judicial decisions" (ch. 1).

**(c) The "improvement of conditions" paraphrase.** Weinfeld offers a positive translation: "the concept refers primarily to the improvement of the conditions of the poor, which is undoubtedly accomplished through regulations issued by the king and his officials, and not by offering legal assistance to the poor man in his litigation with his oppressor" (ch. 1).

**(d) The Akkadian parallel.** The pair corresponds to Akkadian *kittam u mīšaram* ("truth and equity"), and "the execution of righteousness and justice in the royal domain refers primarily to acts on behalf of the poor and less fortunate classes of the people. These were carried out by means of social legislation, initiated by the kings and the ruling circles" (Introduction). The key Akkadian institution is the *mīšarum* edict — royal proclamation of debt cancellation, slave release, and property restoration.

**(e) The individual-passage translation.** In specific passages, Weinfeld translates the phrase expansively: "יַעֲשֶׂה מִשְׁפָּט does not mean to pass a righteous verdict in a court, but rather, to perform deeds of righteousness and truth" (ch. 10).

#### 10.11.2 What Weinfeld Gets Right

Weinfeld's contribution is genuinely important in three respects:

1. **The ANE comparative data is real.** The Akkadian *kittam u mīšaram* word pair and the *mīšarum* edict tradition do provide a genuine ancient Near Eastern institutional context for the Hebrew pair. This is a significant advance over purely intra-biblical word studies.

2. **The royal-administration context is correct.** The word pair is overwhelmingly attested in royal contexts (2 Sam 8:15; 1 Kgs 10:9; Jer 22:3, 15; 23:5; 33:15; Ps 72:1-2; Ezek 45:9). Weinfeld is right that the primary Sitz im Leben is royal governance, not private litigation.

3. **The pair functions as a unit.** The hendiadys observation has lexicographic support: NIDOTTE (Reimer) cites Weinfeld approvingly, noting that "since the pair forms a hendiadys, precise and distinct meanings for each of the partners should not be sought" (NIDOTTE, *tsedaqah* article).

#### 10.11.3 Where Weinfeld's Translation Logic Commits the Catalogued Fallacies

Despite these strengths, Weinfeld's key translation moves commit several of the fallacies identified in Phase 10.1–10.10:

**Fallacy 1: Illegitimate totality transfer applied to the hendiadys itself.**

Weinfeld's hendiadys thesis works like this: because the pair *sometimes* occurs in contexts involving care for the poor (Jer 22:3, 15-16; Isa 1:17; Ps 72:1-4), and because the Akkadian parallel *sometimes* involves debt cancellation, therefore the pair *always* means "social justice" — even in passages where the context is hymnic praise of divine attributes (Ps 33:5; 89:15; 97:2), wisdom instruction (Prov 1:3; 2:9; 8:20; 21:3), or covenantal fidelity (Hos 2:21). This is Barr's "illegitimate totality transfer" applied not to a single word but to a word pair: the meaning of the pair *in certain contexts* is imported into *all occurrences*.

Weinfeld himself acknowledges the difficulty implicitly when he notes that the Rabbis struggled with the double term (citing b. Sanhedrin 6b). But his solution — that the Rabbis were simply wrong to connect it with judicial process — requires dismissing the dominant synchronic meaning that Hebrew speakers apparently heard in the pair throughout the Talmudic period.

**Fallacy 2: False disjunction between "judicial" and "social."**

Weinfeld's sharpest claim is that "the word מִשְׁפָּט in this word-pair should not be understood in the juridical sense." This is Carson's "unwarranted semantic disjunction" (§11): Weinfeld forces a choice between "mere judicial verdict" and "social justice," excluding the possibility that the judicial process *is itself* the mechanism for social protection. The covenant stipulates protection of widow, orphan, and alien (Ex 22:21-24; Dt 24:17-18); the court system is the institution that *enforces* those stipulations. When Jeremiah demands that the king עֲשׂוּ מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה (Jer 22:3), the very next clause specifies: "deliver from the hand of the oppressor him who has been robbed. And do no wrong or violence to the resident alien, the fatherless, and the widow." The content is care for the vulnerable; the mechanism is the judicial-administrative apparatus of the state. Weinfeld's disjunction dissolves precisely what the text holds together.

This matters for translation: if one translates the pair as "social justice" (Weinfeld's preference), the English reader loses the judicial-administrative mechanism that the Hebrew encodes. If one translates it as "justice and righteousness" (ESV, NASB), the judicial-covenantal mechanism remains visible.

**Fallacy 3: Semantic anachronism via the Akkadian parallel.**

Weinfeld's most sophisticated move is to read the Hebrew pair through the lens of Akkadian *mīšarum* edicts — royal proclamations of debt cancellation and slave release. But this creates a semantic anachronism in the opposite direction from Wolterstorff's: instead of reading modern "rights" language back into the text, Weinfeld reads a specific Mesopotamian institutional practice (the *mīšarum* edict) into every occurrence of the Hebrew pair, even in texts that have no connection to debt cancellation or slave release.

When Psalm 33:5 says "He loves צְדָקָה וּמִשְׁפָּט; the earth is full of the חֶסֶד of the LORD," the context is cosmic — God's creative and sustaining activity — not an edict of debt cancellation. When Proverbs 21:3 says "To do צְדָקָה וּמִשְׁפָּט is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice," the context is individual piety, not royal social legislation. Weinfeld's Akkadian parallel illuminates royal-commissioning texts brilliantly but distorts the pair's meaning when applied universally.

Carson's warning about "careless appeal to background material" (§5) is directly relevant: "There may be an inappropriate appeal to background material that does not involve an intrinsically unlikely meaning" — the Akkadian parallel is legitimate background for some occurrences but illegitimate when treated as the master key to all.

**Fallacy 4: Selective evidence in the distinction between מִשְׁפָּט צֶדֶק and מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה.**

Weinfeld's distinction between the two constructions (the construct chain מִשְׁפָּט צֶדֶק = "righteous verdict" vs. the hendiadys מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה = "social justice") is linguistically real — they are different constructions. But Weinfeld uses this distinction to strip *all* juridical meaning from the hendiadys, which does not follow. A hendiadys combining two terms that each independently have judicial meanings does not thereby lose judicial meaning. The English hendiadys "law and order" does not cease to involve law simply because it functions as a unit; "cease and desist" does not lose the meaning of ceasing. Weinfeld's move from "the hendiadys is not *merely* a judicial verdict" to "the hendiadys should not be understood in the juridical sense" is an overreach — from a reasonable observation about the pair's broader-than-forensic scope to an unwarranted exclusion of the forensic sense altogether.

#### 10.11.4 Wolterstorff's Misappropriation of Weinfeld

Wolterstorff's use of Weinfeld compounds these problems by adding a further step that Weinfeld himself never takes:

| Step | Weinfeld | Wolterstorff |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 1 | מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה = hendiadys for "social justice" | Same |
| 2 | Content: royal obligation to improve conditions of the poor through social legislation | Content: "primary justice" = state of affairs in which inherent human rights are satisfied |
| 3 | Mechanism: *mīšarum* edicts — sovereign acts of royal prerogative | Mechanism: recognition and satisfaction of pre-legal rights inherent in persons |
| 4 | ANE parallel: king receives judicial capacity from the gods as divine endowment | ANE parallel: taken as evidence that ancient peoples had a "rights" concept |

The critical gap is between steps 2-3. Weinfeld's framework is *royal-prerogative paternalism*: the king, endowed by the gods with a "sense of justice" (Weinfeld's phrase for Ps 72:1-2), exercises sovereign power to protect the weak. The poor have no *right* to debt cancellation in Weinfeld's Mesopotamian model — the king cancels debts as an act of *grace* when ascending the throne, demonstrating his divinely-granted fitness to rule. Weinfeld himself writes that the Akkadian *mīšarum* edicts were "social reforms" enacted "when [kings] ascended the throne or at other decisive times" — i.e., at the king's initiative and discretion, not in response to the people's rights-claims.

Wolterstorff transforms this royal-paternalism framework into a rights-framework by a move that is entirely philosophical, not lexicographic: he redefines "what the king does for the poor" as "what the poor are *owed*." This is not a translation of Hebrew — it is a philosophical reinterpretation of the social relationship described by the Hebrew, and it commits Carson's "semantic anachronism" (§2) by importing post-Enlightenment rights theory into an ANE royal-prerogative context that both the Hebrew text and Weinfeld's own analysis describe differently.

#### 10.11.5 Translation Implications

The fallacy analysis yields concrete translation guidance:

| Translation Choice | Fallacy Risk | Lexicographic Support |
|---|---|---|
| "social justice" (Weinfeld) | Illegitimate totality transfer; excludes judicial sense; anachronistic English connotation | Partial: supported in royal-commissioning contexts, unsupported in hymnic, wisdom, individual-piety contexts |
| "justice and rights" / "primary justice" (Wolterstorff) | Semantic anachronism; word-concept conflation; no lexicographic attestation for "rights" reading | None: absent from all 6 lexicons and 3 theological dictionaries |
| "justice and righteousness" (ESV, NASB, NRSV) | Low: preserves both judicial (*mishpat*) and relational-normative (*tsedaqah*) senses | Strong: aligns with HALOT, BDB, NIDOTTE, TLOT dominant glosses |
| "just rule" / "righteous governance" (contextual paraphrase) | Low in royal contexts; loses specificity of individual terms | Moderate: captures the royal-administration Sitz im Leben Weinfeld identifies |

The standard English translation "justice and righteousness" preserves the semantic range of both terms without committing to a philosophical framework the Hebrew does not encode. It allows the reader to hear what the lexicographic tradition hears: *mishpat* as judicial-administrative process, *tsedaqah* as conformity to covenant norms — held together in a word pair that describes the faithful administration of God's order, whether by king, judge, or individual Israelite.

Weinfeld's "social justice" translation, while capturing something real about the royal-commissioning context, commits the error Barr diagnosed: it takes the meaning of the pair in a subset of its occurrences and reads it into the word pair as its permanent semantic load. Wolterstorff's "primary justice" / "inherent rights" translation goes further, replacing the Hebrew semantic field entirely with a philosophical framework that is absent from the lexicographic tradition, absent from the ANE parallels (which describe royal prerogative, not popular rights), and absent from Weinfeld's own work — which Wolterstorff cites as his primary evidence.

## Appendix A: Hermeneia Amos Commentary Extracts (Shalom Paul)

### Amos 2:6-8

Paul's commentary on social crimes: selling the righteous for silver and the needy for sandals. Treatment of debt slavery and perversion of legal process. Economic exploitation described through legal/judicial vocabulary — the "righteous" (צַדִּיק) is the innocent party in a lawsuit.

### Amos 5:7

Paul on the reversal of justice: "Those who turn justice to wormwood." The hiphil participle הַהֹפְכִים indicates active perversion of the judicial process. Wormwood (לַעֲנָה) = bitter, poisonous — the court system has become toxic.

### Amos 5:10-15

Paul on gate (שַׁעַר) as locus of judicial proceedings: "They hate the one who reproves in the gate" (v. 10). The gate is the place of legal arbitration, not economic activity. "Trample on the poor" (v. 11) describes exploitation through perverted legal processes.

### Amos 5:21-24

Paul's climactic treatment: the contrast between cultic worship and judicial righteousness. The water/stream imagery (v. 24) emphasizes not quantity of charity but permanence and reliability of just legal process.
